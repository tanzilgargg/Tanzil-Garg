# CYCORA playtest kit

A short plan for watching real people play. Five players will find most of the big problems. Each session takes 30 to 40 minutes.

## Who to recruit

Aim for a mix. The cases the automated tests can't cover matter most:

- someone who types slowly or looks at the keyboard (under about 20 words per minute)
- someone who plays on a phone or tablet
- someone who has never played a typing game
- if CYCORA is aimed at young people, at least two people from that age group
- if you can, someone who uses a screen reader or keyboard-only navigation

Under-16s need a parent or guardian's consent, and the parent should be nearby during the session. Your university will probably want you to use its own consent form. City St George's has an ethics process for student research, so check whether a playtest for a module needs approval before you book anyone.

## Before each session

- Open the game in a **fresh private/incognito window** (or Settings → Reset all progress) so every player starts from onboarding.
- Turn the sound on.
- Have something to take notes on. Screen recording helps if the player agrees.
- Tell them: "We're testing the game, not you. If something is confusing, that's the game's fault. Please think out loud as you play."

## Tasks

Give one task at a time. Don't explain how to do it. If they're stuck for more than a minute, note it and give a small hint.

| # | Task (say this) | It worked if... | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "Get started and set yourself up." | They finish onboarding without help. | Do they read Cy's lines or click straight past? Do they pick an avatar? |
| 2 | "Play your first lesson." | They start lesson 1 and finish it. | Do they find the home row? Do they notice the keyboard guide? How do they react to a wrong key? |
| 3 | "Keep going until something new is added to the restaurant." | They link lessons to the pantry filling up. | Do they notice the room changing? Ask: "What do you think made that appear?" |
| 4 | "Try the Daily Special and then a race." | They finish both. | Is the race penalty (0.4s per mistake) understood? Is the ghost racer confusing? |
| 5 | (Turn on demo mode first.) "Play a recycling lesson." | They make choices and read the feedback. | Do they read the "why" text or skip it? Do they get anything wrong, and does the explanation help? |
| 6 | "Buy something for the restaurant and find it." | They buy an item and spot it in the restaurant. | Is it obvious where it went? Do they understand the Planet bonus on eco items? |
| 7 | "Imagine you're moving to a new laptop. Keep your progress safe." | They find Settings → Download backup. | Where do they look first? |

## Questions afterwards

Ask these out loud and write down what they say in their own words:

1. What was the game about, in one sentence?
2. Which part did you enjoy most? Which part was annoying?
3. Was anything too fast, too slow, or unfair?
4. Did you learn anything about recycling or food waste you didn't know? What?
5. Did any of the characters stand out? Which one, and why?
6. Would you play again tomorrow? What would make you come back?

Then two quick ratings from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very):

- How easy was it to understand what to do?
- How much did you enjoy it?

## Collecting their numbers (optional, with permission)

At the end, ask the player if you can keep their game stats. If they agree, open Settings → **Download backup** and save the file under a code like `P3.json` rather than their name. The file holds their speed, accuracy, lessons played, mistakes in sorting and rescue lessons, and race results. It holds nothing personal except the chef name they typed.

## Writing it up

One row per problem works well:

| Problem | Who hit it (P1–P5) | How bad (1 annoying, 2 slows them down, 3 stops them) | Idea for a fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | |

Fix the 3s first. Anything that more than two people hit is worth fixing even if it's only a 1.
